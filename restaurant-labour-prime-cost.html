Understand restaurant labour and prime cost

Labour cost can rise because rates increase, hours increase or sales fall. Prime cost combines food and beverage cost with labour to show how much revenue these major operating costs consume. Neither ratio identifies the cause on its own.

By Kitchen Pilot Consulting · Updated September 23, 2026

Calculate comparable costs

Labour cost % = total labour cost ÷ net sales × 100
Prime cost = food and beverage cost of goods sold + total labour cost
Prime cost % = prime cost ÷ net sales × 100
Sales per labour hour = net sales ÷ total worked hours

Include employer payroll costs and benefits in labour, alongside wages, salaries and overtime, using the same treatment each period. Identify unpaid owner work separately so it is not mistaken for permanently free capacity. Align payroll and sales dates; include salaried hours consistently when comparing productivity.

Illustration: $80,000 net sales, $25,600 food and beverage cost and $28,000 burdened labour produce $53,600 prime cost, or 67%. With 1,600 worked hours, sales per labour hour are $50. This does not establish whether the restaurant is profitable: rent and other operating expenses remain. Use the prime-cost calculator.

Separate the wage, hours and sales effects

Suppose labour stays at $28,000 while sales fall from $80,000 to $70,000. Labour percentage increases from 35% to 40% without any increase in labour spending. The useful next question is why sales fell and how staffing responds to demand, not simply who scheduled too many people.

Compare hours by role and daypart, average paid rate, overtime, opening hours, covers and average spend. A sales increase driven only by higher prices can improve sales per labour hour without making the team physically more productive.

Check the work before cutting hours

  • Compare scheduled and actual hours with reservations, recent demand and planned prep.
  • Look for overtime causes: late prep, absences, skill gaps or repeated rework.
  • Check whether shift overlaps match the busiest stations rather than only total covers.
  • Measure searching, unnecessary movement and equipment queues with the team.
  • Consider service quality, safety and required coverage alongside any proposed reduction.

A shorter prep list, clearer handoff or changed station setup may address a bottleneck more effectively than a blanket staffing cut. See the operations and workflow review.

Choose targets that fit the restaurant

A labour percentage from a different service model is not automatically an appropriate target. Wage market, opening hours, owner involvement, menu complexity and service expectations matter. Establish your own baseline and use a consistent definition before comparing outside benchmarks.

Kitchen Pilot reviews labour alongside food costs and the wider profitability picture. A practical action plan specifies a change, an owner and a review date, then compares the result over similar trading periods. Prime cost is a diagnostic measure, not a promised profit outcome.
