Actual vs theoretical food cost

A food-cost gap tells you where to investigate. It does not, by itself, tell you what went wrong or how much you can recover.

By Kitchen Pilot Consulting · Restaurant operations guide

1. Start with comparable records

Use one reporting period and consistent food categories. Check that opening and closing inventory are valued consistently, supplier credits are included and food sales exclude sales tax. Separate beverage sales if they are not included in the food-cost calculation. Record relevant transfers and adjustments rather than silently mixing categories.

2. Calculate actual food cost

For a simple operation with no transfers or other adjustments:

Opening food inventory + net food purchases − closing food inventory = food used

Food used ÷ net food sales × 100 = actual food-cost percentage

Suppose opening inventory is $4,000, net purchases are $12,000 and closing inventory is $3,500. Food used is $12,500. With $40,000 in net food sales, actual food cost is 31.25%. These are illustrative amounts in a single currency, not a benchmark or a client result.

3. Estimate theoretical food cost

Multiply each item’s standard ingredient cost by the number sold, then total the results. Recipes should include measured yields, portions, sides and modifiers. Use a consistent costing basis for a meaningful comparison.

If those recipes and sales imply $11,200 of food use against the same $40,000 in food sales, theoretical food cost is 28%. The difference is $1,300, or 3.25 percentage points. This is not the same as a 3.25% relative increase.

Actual-versus-theoretical reporting compares expected usage with recorded usage; Restaurant365’s food variance documentation also describes investigating differences in quantities and dollars.

4. Check the inputs before blaming the operation

  • Inventory: Were all storage locations counted at the same point in the operating cycle? Were units and valuations consistent?
  • Purchases: Were pack sizes, credits, substitutions and period cutoffs recorded correctly?
  • Recipes: Are current ingredients, batch yields, portions and modifiers reflected?
  • Sales: Are item mappings complete? How are discounts, voids, comps and staff meals treated?
  • Production: What evidence exists for waste, remakes, overproduction or portion inconsistency?

A difference can contain legitimate non-sales usage, measurement errors and operational loss. A spreadsheet gap alone cannot establish theft or prove that the whole amount is recoverable.

Illustrative audit finding: portion consistency

Observation: A sauce recipe assumes 60 grams per portion. A hypothetical spot check finds an average of 75 grams across the portions measured.

Possible implication: At a usable sauce cost of $8 per kilogram, the extra 15 grams costs $0.12 per portion. At 1,000 comparable portions, that would be $120 of additional ingredient use.

What to verify: Whether the sample represents normal service, the recipe reflects the intended guest portion, and the yield and sales count are correct.

Practical action: Confirm the intended portion, match the serving tool to it and repeat the measurement after the team has a clear standard.

Follow-up measure: Portion weights and sauce use per recorded sale over comparable periods.

This example demonstrates reasoning. It is not an actual customer finding, promised saving or complete audit deliverable.

5. Track the cause, not just the headline percentage

Keep a short record of each finding, the evidence, the person responsible for the next action and the date to review it. Change a measurable practice and check comparable periods. A percentage can improve because the sales mix changes even when waste does not.

For related decisions, see why menu contribution differs from food-cost percentage. If the numbers are incomplete or the gap persists, a food-cost review can help establish what needs to be measured next.
