Kitchen Pilot / Practical restaurant analysis

Why is your restaurant busy but not profitable?

Strong sales can still leave little profit when the cost of producing those sales, running the operation and covering overhead consumes the revenue. Start with a consistent profit-and-loss statement, then investigate the largest unexplained differences.

By Kitchen Pilot Consulting · Updated September 23, 2026

Start with the right question

Low profit and low cash are related, but they are not the same problem. A profitable month can leave little cash after equipment purchases, loan principal repayments or inventory growth. Conversely, borrowing can increase the bank balance without creating profit. Compare your P&L with cash movements and ask your bookkeeper to reconcile timing and classifications.

Read the restaurant P&L in layers

Use net sales after discounts and refunds, excluding sales tax, and expenses for the same period. Keep food and beverage categories consistent. Gross profit is sales less the cost of goods sold; it is not money available to withdraw.

Gross profit = net sales − cost of goods sold
Gross margin % = gross profit ÷ net sales × 100
Operating margin % = operating profit ÷ net sales × 100

Operating profit deducts operating expenses, including labour and overhead, before interest and income tax. Classification matters: do not subtract a cost twice if your accounts already include it in cost of sales.

Illustration: $100,000 net sales minus $32,000 cost of goods leaves $68,000 gross profit. After $35,000 labour and $28,000 other operating expenses, operating profit is $5,000 and operating margin is 5%. These are teaching figures, not industry targets or client results.

Find which part needs investigation

  • Sales and menu mix: compare items, discounts, channels and dayparts. A shift towards low-contribution sales can leave you busier without improving the result.
  • Food costs: compare inventory-based usage with recipe-based expectations. Follow the food-cost variance investigation.
  • Labour: separate wage-rate changes, hours and sales effects. Review labour and prime-cost analysis.
  • Stock loss: distinguish purchasing, stock accumulation and usage. Use the waste and inventory controls guide.
  • Pricing: examine dollar contribution as well as cost percentage. See menu profitability and pricing.
  • Overhead: review occupancy, utilities, subscriptions, repairs and channel fees against the accounts. Separate recurring costs from exceptional items.

Check contribution and break-even sales

Contribution is revenue left after variable costs. Unlike ingredient contribution on a menu sheet, a business break-even model must include every cost treated as variable: for example ingredients, payment fees, packaging and the genuinely variable part of labour. Fixed salaries belong with fixed costs in that model.

Contribution margin ratio = (sales − variable costs) ÷ sales
Break-even sales = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio

If monthly fixed costs are $30,000 and the variable-cost ratio is 60%, contribution is 40% and break-even sales are $75,000. This assumes stable prices, sales mix and cost behaviour within the relevant capacity range. It is an operating model, not a cash forecast. Try the break-even calculator.
