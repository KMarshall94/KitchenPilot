One restaurant, one connected business

A food-cost problem can begin in purchasing, recipe data or service execution. A labour problem can begin in demand, scheduling or workflow. We do not assume that ownership, management, kitchen staff or front-of-house staff are at fault. A useful diagnosis follows the numbers and operating evidence.

  1. Define the concern: agree the decision to support, the reporting period, available records and scope.
  2. Validate the baseline: reconcile units, dates, sales categories and cost definitions. Mark estimates and missing inputs.
  3. Trace the difference: connect revenue, menu mix, pricing, food cost, purchasing, inventory, waste, labour deployment, scheduling and productivity.
  4. Check the operation: examine prep, workflow, handoffs, management routines and financial controls. Distinguish observation from an untested explanation.
  5. Choose corrective action: prioritize by evidence, likely impact and practical effort. Assign responsibility for the action, not blame for an unproven cause.
  6. Review comparable results: agree measures and a review date. Record changes in volume, prices or menu mix that affect interpretation.

Restaurant profitability and operational audit checklist

Use this checklist to prepare a review. Missing records are findings to resolve, not a reason to invent precision.

  • Revenue: net sales by daypart and channel; discounts, refunds, covers and average spend.
  • Menu: current recipes, measured yields, portions, prices, modifiers and item-level sales.
  • Purchasing: invoices, credits, substitutions, pack sizes and approval routines.
  • Inventory and waste: opening/closing counts, transfers, stock valuation and reason-based waste logs.
  • Labour: schedules, actual hours, payroll burden, overtime, roles and demand assumptions.
  • Productivity: prep completion, sales per labour hour, ticket patterns and recurring queues.
  • Operating expenses: occupancy, utilities, subscriptions, repairs, transaction fees and unusual items.
  • Management systems: who checks variances, how issues reach the next shift and how changes are followed up.
  • Financial controls: reconciliation of sales and deposits, stock and invoice cutoffs, purchasing approvals and consistent P&L categories.
  • Action record: evidence, confidence, recommended change, responsible person and review date.

What a useful finding looks like

A finding explains what was measured, why it matters and what remains uncertain. It proposes an action and a way to evaluate it. Our illustrative portion-variance finding shows this structure without presenting it as a client result.
