Menu profitability consulting

A popular dish is not automatically a profitable one. Understand what each item contributes, how often it sells and what it asks of your kitchen.

Look beyond the food-cost percentage

A percentage describes the relationship between ingredient cost and selling price. It does not tell you how many dollars remain from a sale or how much preparation and service capacity the dish uses. A menu review needs both item economics and operational context.

Kitchen Pilot reviews recipe costing, selling prices and item sales to help identify where menu decisions deserve attention. The starting point is a costed recipe that reflects what is actually served.

Build a complete cost per portion

Small omissions add up: cooking oil, sauces, garnishes, side dishes and modifiers all need consistent treatment. Batch recipes need a measured output, and ingredients bought by weight need a usable yield where appropriate. A theoretical recipe is only useful if its units and portion assumptions are clear.

Delivery fees, packaging and other channel-specific costs should be considered separately where relevant. Comparing a dine-in sale with a delivery sale without accounting for their different costs can lead to the wrong conclusion.

Dish B has a higher food-cost percentage but leaves more dollars after ingredients on each sale. That does not automatically make it the better item: sales volume, other variable costs, labour demands and kitchen capacity still matter. Neither figure is net profit.

Use sales mix to prioritize decisions

Reviewing costs without sales volume can put attention on a dish that rarely sells while overlooking a small cost issue repeated hundreds of times. Looking at contribution and popularity together helps focus the review.

  • Which high-volume items have incomplete or outdated recipe costs?
  • Which dishes contribute useful dollars but create a service bottleneck?
  • Where do similar items require separate prep or ingredients that move slowly?
  • What effect could a portion, price or recipe change have on guest value?

Make changes with a way to evaluate them

A useful recommendation identifies the reason for a change and what to monitor afterward. Depending on the finding, that could mean recipe consistency, sales mix, ingredient usage or ticket times. Blanket price increases and removing every high-percentage item are not substitutes for analysis.
