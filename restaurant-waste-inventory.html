Kitchen Pilot / Practical restaurant analysis

Find the cause of restaurant waste and stock variance

Reliable counts show what stock was used. A waste log helps explain part of that usage. Combine both with recipes, sales and observation before deciding whether the issue is purchasing, production, portions or recording.

By Kitchen Pilot Consulting · Updated September 23, 2026

Separate purchases from usage

Inventory usage = opening inventory + net purchases + transfers in − transfers out − closing inventory

Value every term on a consistent basis and align cutoffs. Supplier credits reduce net purchases. Inventory bought for next week belongs in closing stock if it is still present; it is not automatically this week’s food use. Count all storage areas, including prepared batches, using agreed units and recipe-based values where needed.

Illustration: opening stock of $3,000 plus $9,000 net purchases less $2,500 closing stock gives $9,500 usage, assuming no transfers. If a $600 delivery is counted in closing stock but its invoice is omitted from purchases, recorded usage will be understated.

Make waste records useful

Record the item, quantity, unit, reason and date. Separate spoilage, trim, overproduction, remakes and plate returns. Expected preparation loss should already be reflected in usable recipe yield; do not automatically call every trim loss preventable waste.

Recorded waste cost as % of usage = recorded waste cost ÷ inventory usage cost × 100

If recorded waste costs $285 against $9,500 of usage, this measure is 3%. It is only the waste actually recorded. State the denominator: waste divided by purchases or sales is a different measure and cannot be compared directly. Do not subtract waste again from inventory usage when calculating actual food cost; that usage already includes stock that was discarded.

Connect each cause to a control

  • Spoilage: check delivery condition, date labels, rotation and order quantities against demand.
  • Overproduction: compare prep quantities with sales by daypart and revise batch sizes.
  • Yield loss: weigh incoming product and usable output; check recipe assumptions and supplier specifications.
  • Portion variance: compare actual portions with intended standards and serving tools.
  • Count errors: use a consistent count sheet and investigate unusual movements in a few high-value items first.

Keep the process workable during service. A brief reason-based log that gets used is more informative than a complex sheet no one completes.

What an unexplained difference means

Sales recipes imply expected usage; inventory records show actual usage. The difference can include waste, staff meals, comps, transfers, recipe errors and measurement problems. It does not prove theft or indicate that the entire amount can be recovered. Follow our actual versus theoretical food-cost guide to investigate it.
