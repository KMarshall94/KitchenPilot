Restaurant food cost consulting

When the food-cost number keeps moving, start by finding out what changed. Price, quantity, yield and recording errors need different responses.

Find the reason behind a rising food cost

Buying food and using food are different events. An unusually large order can increase purchasing for a week without increasing the food used in that week. Inconsistent inventory counts can make the reverse appear true. Before recommending a fix, the review needs a consistent period and a reliable view of stock movement.

Kitchen Pilot examines the inputs behind the number: invoices, inventory records, recipes, standard portions and item sales. We connect those inputs to practical questions your team can investigate.

What we look at

  • Purchasing: pack sizes, unit prices, substitutions, credits and how ordering decisions are made.
  • Inventory: count timing, storage locations, measurement units and stock that may be missed or counted twice.
  • Recipe costing: current ingredient costs, batch output, trim and cooking yields, garnishes and modifiers.
  • Portioning: whether the written standard matches the serving tools and portions used during service.
  • Waste: spoilage, overproduction, preparation loss, remakes and the consistency of the waste record.

Variance is a starting point, not a verdict

Theoretical food cost estimates ingredient use from recipes and recorded sales. Actual cost reflects stock movement. A gap can come from several sources, including incomplete recipes or data errors. It should not automatically be treated as staff misconduct or entirely recoverable waste.

For example, if a supplier changes a case from 10 kilograms to 8 kilograms but the costing sheet still assumes 10, your unit cost is understated. Changing portion sizes would not correct the underlying data problem.

From a finding to a workable control

The appropriate action depends on the cause. It might be updating a pack-size conversion, weighing a batch to verify yield, clarifying a portion standard, recording waste by reason or counting a high-value ingredient consistently. A control is more useful when someone can own it and repeat it during a normal shift.
